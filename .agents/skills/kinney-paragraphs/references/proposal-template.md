# Full research proposal

Once a Kinney document clears Step II/III as a good bet, it expands into a full
proposal. The Kinney spine maps directly onto the longer format:

| Section | Purpose | Maps to |
|---|---|---|
| **Title** | Concise and informative — names X, Y, and setting. | — |
| **Abstract** | Problem, objectives, expected outcomes, one paragraph. | Whole document |
| **Issues** | Why the problem is interesting and important. | Why |
| **Objectives** | The specific questions the study answers. | What |
| **Literature** | Relevant work organized *by theme*, not paper by paper. | What / Why background |
| **Method** | The how and why of the process. **Must address the alternatives** considered. | How |
| **Benefits** | Anticipated outcomes — contribution to **policy and practice**, not only literature. | Why |

## Template

```markdown
# [Title — names X, Y, and setting]

## Abstract
[One paragraph: the problem, the objectives, the expected outcomes.]

## Issues
[Why this problem is interesting and important. The tension, puzzle, or policy
stakes.]

## Objectives
[The specific research questions, and how answering them addresses the issue.]

## Literature
[Relevant publications organized by theme. Per theme: what is known, what is
contested, what gap this study fills.]

## Method
[Setting, sample, data, measurement, identification. Explicitly address the
alternatives considered and justify why the chosen design is appropriate.]

## Benefits
[What makes the study worthwhile. Contribution to policy and practice, beyond
the academic contribution.]
```

## The alternatives requirement

A proposal that names only its chosen design is weaker than one that shows the
design space and argues for its choice. For each major decision — identification
strategy, sample, key proxy — state the alternatives considered and why the
selected option dominates *for this question*. This is the How-PV table rendered
as prose, and referees and committees both look for it.
